Introduction

The continuum of Lecture 3 is the smooth canvas; a spectral line is a sharp spike of extra opacity at one wavelength, where a bound electron jumps between two specific energy levels. Where that opacity rises, the τ=2/3\tau = 2/3τ=2/3 surface moves up to cooler, dimmer layers, and the line appears in absorption. Two questions define a line. How strong is it — how much opacity does it add at line centre? And what shape does it have — how is that opacity spread over wavelength?

The strength is set by the transition's oscillator strength and the number of atoms in the lower level; the shape, by the Voigt profile, the convolution of a thermal Doppler core with pressure-broadened Lorentzian wings. We will lean on results already in hand — the stimulated-emission factor from Lecture 1 and the Boltzmann lower-level population from Lecture 2 — and spend our effort on what is genuinely new here: the line strength carried by log⁡gf\log gfloggf, the Voigt profile, and the damping budget that fills its wings. This lecture builds one line, end to end, on the solar atmosphere we constructed; the next assembles the million-line forest.

def compare(name, ours, ref, tol=1e-6):
    """Report the worst relative difference; 'exact' = machine precision, 'agree' = within tol."""
    ours, ref = np.asarray(ours, float), np.asarray(ref, float)
    denom = np.where(ref != 0.0, np.abs(ref), 1.0)          # avoid divide-by-zero where ref==0
    rel = float(np.max(np.abs(ours - ref) / denom))
    tag = "exact" if rel < 1e-12 else ("agree" if rel < tol else "CHECK")
    print(f"{name:30s}  max|rel diff| = {rel:.2e}   [{tag}]")
    return rel

The strength of a line

The monochromatic absorption coefficient of a bound-bound transition, per unit volume, is

αν=πe2mec fℓu nℓ ϕ(ν) (1−e−hν/kT)[cm−1],\alpha_\nu = \frac{\pi e^2}{m_e c}\, f_{\ell u}\, n_\ell\, \phi(\nu)\,\big(1 - e^{-h\nu/kT}\big)\quad[\mathrm{cm^{-1}}],αν​=me​cπe2​fℓu​nℓ​ϕ(ν)(1−e−hν/kT)[cm−1],

a product of four pieces. The classical constant πe2/mec=0.02654 cm2 Hz\pi e^2/m_e c = 0.02654\ \mathrm{cm^2\,Hz}πe2/me​c=0.02654 cm2Hz is the cross-section a classical oscillator would present, integrated over frequency. The oscillator strength fℓuf_{\ell u}fℓu​ — a dimensionless quantum-mechanical number, usually of order unity or smaller but not strictly bounded by 1 — corrects that classical value for the real transition. The lower-level population nℓn_\ellnℓ​ counts how many absorbers are available. And ϕ(ν)\phi(\nu)ϕ(ν) is the line profile, normalised to ∫ϕ dν=1\int\phi\,d\nu = 1∫ϕdν=1, which spreads the opacity over frequency. The last factor is the same stimulated-emission correction (1−e−hν/kT)(1 - e^{-h\nu/kT})(1−e−hν/kT) we met in Lecture 1; nothing new here.

Line lists do not tabulate fff directly; they give log⁡gf\log gfloggf, the base-ten logarithm of the oscillator strength times the lower level's statistical weight gℓg_\ellgℓ​. The combination is what appears in the opacity, because fℓu nℓ=gf⋅(nℓ/gℓ)f_{\ell u}\,n_\ell = gf \cdot (n_\ell/g_\ell)fℓu​nℓ​=gf⋅(nℓ​/gℓ​), and nℓ/gℓn_\ell/g_\ellnℓ​/gℓ​ is exactly what the Boltzmann factor gives without needing gℓg_\ellgℓ​ separately. Thus the tabulated gfgfgf is not a new physical factor; it is a bookkeeping device that pairs naturally with the Boltzmann population per sublevel, nℓ/gℓn_\ell/g_\ellnℓ​/gℓ​. So log⁡gf\log gfloggf is the single number that sets a line's intrinsic strength — a strong line might have log⁡gf≈0\log gf \approx 0loggf≈0, a weak one log⁡gf≈−5\log gf \approx -5loggf≈−5.

Populating the lower level

How many atoms sit in the lower level of our transition? Two factors set it, both from Lecture 2. First, what fraction of the element is in the right ionization stage — here neutral iron, Fe I. Second, within that ion, the Boltzmann factor places a fraction in the lower level ℓ\ellℓ at excitation energy χℓ\chi_\ellχℓ​ above the ground state:

nℓgℓ=n(Fe I)UFe I(T) e−χℓ/kT.\frac{n_\ell}{g_\ell} = \frac{n(\mathrm{Fe\,I})}{U_{\rm Fe\,I}(T)}\,e^{-\chi_\ell/kT}.gℓ​nℓ​​=UFeI​(T)n(FeI)​e−χℓ​/kT.

The excitation potential χℓ\chi_\ellχℓ​ is decisive: a line from the ground state (χℓ=0\chi_\ell = 0χℓ​=0) is available everywhere, while a line from a level several eV up is populated only in the hotter, deeper layers — which is why lines of different excitation potential probe different depths, the lever that lets spectroscopy measure temperature. We take n(Fe I)n(\mathrm{Fe\,I})n(FeI) and UFe IU_{\rm Fe\,I}UFeI​ straight from the equation of state of Lecture 2, carried in the reference data; nothing here is re-derived.

We pull the atmospheric structure out of the reference bundle and pick the layer nearest τ=2/3\tau = 2/3τ=2/3 — the "surface" the continuum emerges from — as the reference depth for the rest of the lecture. The shape: Doppler core and Lorentzian wings

The profile ϕ(ν)\phi(\nu)ϕ(ν) has two origins. Thermal motion Doppler-shifts each atom's absorption; averaged over a Maxwellian (plus a microturbulent velocity ξ\xiξ that represents unresolved small-scale flows), this gives a Gaussian core of 1/e1/e1/e half-width

ΔνD=ν0c2kTm+ξ2.\Delta\nu_D = \frac{\nu_0}{c}\sqrt{\frac{2kT}{m} + \xi^2}.ΔνD​=cν0​​m2kT​+ξ2​.

Independently, the finite lifetime of the atomic levels and perturbations from passing particles broaden the line into a Lorentzian, with a damping rate γ=γrad+γStark+γvdW\gamma = \gamma_{\rm rad} + \gamma_{\rm Stark} + \gamma_{\rm vdW}γ=γrad​+γStark​+γvdW​ (in s−1\mathrm{s^{-1}}s−1): natural broadening (the radiative lifetime, always present), Stark broadening (collisions with charged particles, leading dependence ∝ne\propto n_e∝ne​), and van der Waals broadening (collisions with neutral hydrogen, leading dependence ∝nH\propto n_{\rm H}∝nH​, dominant in cool dwarfs). Here we emphasise the dominant density dependence; real line lists carry transition-specific broadening constants and temperature dependences, introduced in Lecture 5. Convolving the Gaussian core with the Lorentzian wings gives the Voigt profile

ϕ(ν)=1π ΔνD H(a,v),v=ν−ν0ΔνD,a=γ4π ΔνD,\phi(\nu) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Delta\nu_D}\,H(a, v),\qquad v = \frac{\nu - \nu_0}{\Delta\nu_D},\qquad a = \frac{\gamma}{4\pi\,\Delta\nu_D},ϕ(ν)=π​ΔνD​1​H(a,v),v=ΔνD​ν−ν0​​,a=4πΔνD​γ​,

where vvv measures the distance from line centre in Doppler widths and the damping parameter aaa is the ratio of Lorentzian to Doppler width. (The 4π4\pi4π is not arbitrary: γ/4π\gamma/4\piγ/4π is exactly the Lorentzian half-width at half-maximum in ordinary frequency space, so aaa is that half-width measured in Doppler widths.) The dimensionless Voigt function H(a,v)H(a,v)H(a,v) is the heart of every line.

From the exact Voigt to Kurucz's compatibility kernel

H(a,v)H(a,v)H(a,v) is, mathematically, the real part of the Faddeeva function w(z)=e−z2erfc(−iz)w(z) = e^{-z^2}\mathrm{erfc}(-iz)w(z)=e−z2erfc(−iz) at z=v+iaz = v + iaz=v+ia, i.e. H(a,v)=Re w(v+ia)H(a,v) = \mathrm{Re}\,w(v+ia)H(a,v)=Rew(v+ia). Evaluating it exactly — for example with scipy.special.wofz — is one option, and swapping that in to compare is a good exercise. But the standard in this book is to reproduce the reference exactly, and the reference does not use the Faddeeva function: it uses Kurucz's fast Harris-series approximation,

H(a,v)≈H0(v)+a H1(v)+a2H2(v)(near line centre),H(a,v) \approx H_0(v) + a\,H_1(v) + a^2 H_2(v) \quad\text{(near line centre)},H(a,v)≈H0​(v)+aH1​(v)+a2H2​(v)(near line centre),

built from three precomputed tables — H0(v)=e−v2H_0(v) = e^{-v^2}H0​(v)=e−v2, H1(v)H_1(v)H1​(v), and H2(v)=(1−2v2)e−v2H_2(v) = (1-2v^2)e^{-v^2}H2​(v)=(1−2v2)e−v2 — with a Lorentzian-wing form far from centre and an intermediate branch between. These tables are numerical reference values for a special function, not atomic data like wavelengths or gfgfgf values: they depend only on the dimensionless coordinate vvv, never on the element. We reuse them and reimplement the routine's exact branch logic, so our Voigt matches the gold standard to machine precision. (The Harris approximation itself differs from the true Faddeeva by up to ∼1%\sim1\%∼1% at large damping — a property of the reference we are matching, not an error of ours.)

The next two functions are not meant to derive the Harris approximation; they are a compatibility kernel that reproduces Kurucz's branch choices and coefficients exactly, so the comparison is bit-for-bit. The numeric constants are his, to be trusted rather than memorised; the only physical inputs are the damping parameter aaa and the reduced frequency vvv. We first define the asymptotic Lorentzian-wing branch on its own, then the dispatcher that selects a branch by the size of aaa.

[4]Python 
h0tab, h1tab, h2tab = REF["h0tab"], REF["h1tab"], REF["h2tab"]   # Harris special-function tables (math, not atomic data)

def _voigt_wing(a, v):
    """Asymptotic (Lorentzian-wing) branch of Kurucz's Voigt approximation, valid far from centre."""
    aa, vv = a*a, v*v
    u = (aa + vv)*1.4142                                     # sqrt(2)*(a^2+v^2); sets the wing scale
    val = a*0.79788/u                                        # leading Lorentzian term, 0.79788 ~ 1/(sqrt(2)*sqrt(pi))*... (Kurucz const)
    if a <= 100.0:                                           # higher-order correction (skipped at huge damping)
        aau, vvu, uu = aa/u, vv/u, u*u
        val = ((((aau - 10.0*vvu)*aau*3.0 + 15.0*vvu*vvu) + 3.0*vv - aa)/uu + 1.0)*val
    return val

The dispatcher below first maps each ∣v∣|v|∣v∣ to the nearest table index (the tables are sampled every 0.0050.0050.005 in vvv, hence the factor 200200200), then picks one of three regimes by the damping aaa: a second-order Harris series for weak damping, the pure asymptotic wing for strong damping, and a blended branch in between that switches per point at a+∣v∣=3.2a + |v| = 3.2a+∣v∣=3.2.

[5]Python 
def voigt_H(a, v):
    """Voigt H(a,v): Kurucz's Harris-table routine, reproduced exactly (the gold-standard form)."""
    v = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(v, float)); av = np.abs(v)
    iv = np.clip((av*200.0 + 0.5).astype(int), 0, h0tab.size-1)   # nearest table index (200 = 1/0.005 grid step)
    H0, H1, H2 = h0tab[iv], h1tab[iv], h2tab[iv]             # look up the three Harris coefficients
    out = np.empty_like(v)
    if a < 0.2:                                              # weak damping: 2nd-order Harris series
        far = av > 10.0                                      # very far out, fall back to a bare Lorentzian wing
        out[far]  = 0.5642*a/(v[far]*v[far])
        out[~far] = (H2[~far]*a + H1[~far])*a + H0[~far]     # H0 + a*H1 + a^2*H2 (Horner form)
    elif a > 1.4:                                            # strong damping: pure asymptotic wing everywhere
        out[:] = _voigt_wing(a, v)
    else:                                                    # intermediate: split per point at a+|v|=3.2
        asy = (a + av) > 3.2                                 # points far enough out use the wing branch
        out[asy] = _voigt_wing(a, v[asy])
        m = ~asy; vv = v[m]*v[m]; h0 = H0[m]; h1t = H1[m]; h2t = H2[m]   # remaining points: blended polynomial
        h1 = h1t + h0*1.12838                                # 1.12838 ~ 2/sqrt(pi): Kurucz recurrence constants
        h2 = h2t + h1*1.12838 - h0
        h3 = (1.0 - h2t)*0.37613 - h1*0.66667*vv + h2*1.12838
        h4 = (3.0*h3 - h1)*0.37613 + h0*0.66667*vv*vv
        polyA = (((h4*a + h3)*a + h2)*a + h1)*a + h0         # quartic in a, coefficients h0..h4
        polyB = ((-0.122727278*a + 0.532770573)*a - 0.96284325)*a + 0.979895032   # cubic correction in a
        out[m] = polyA*polyB
    return out

Assembling one iron line

Now put it together for a representative neutral-iron line in our window: rest wavelength λ0=500.5 nm\lambda_0 = 500.5\ \mathrm{nm}λ0​=500.5 nm, log⁡gf=−1.0\log gf = -1.0loggf=−1.0, lower-level excitation χℓ=3.3 eV\chi_\ell = 3.3\ \mathrm{eV}χℓ​=3.3 eV. We build its opacity profile at the photosphere, with a microturbulence ξ=2 km s−1\xi = 2\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}ξ=2 kms−1 and a damping rate dominated by radiation and van der Waals collisions. The result is the line absorption coefficient per gram, αν/ρ\alpha_\nu/\rhoαν​/ρ, which we overlay on the continuous opacity of Lecture 3 — the spike that carves the line.

The line raises the opacity more than a hundredfold at its centre, falling through a Gaussian core into Lorentzian wings that merge back into the continuum a fraction of an ångström away. At line centre the τ=2/3\tau=2/3τ=2/3 surface is pushed up into cool layers and the emergent flux drops — that conversion of opacity into a flux profile is the radiative transfer of Lecture 7. For now, note the levers we have exposed: log⁡gf\log gfloggf and the Boltzmann factor scale the whole profile up or down, while the damping aaa controls how much of the line lives in the wings. Vary log⁡gf\log gfloggf (or the abundance hidden inside n(Fe I)n(\mathrm{Fe\,I})n(FeI)) and you trace the curve of growth — the relation between a line's strength and its equivalent width that turns a measured depth into an abundance.

Synthesis: what you built and where it goes

You built a single spectral line from its parts. Its strength is the classical constant times the oscillator strength log gf times the lower-level population — and that population comes from the ionization (Saha) and excitation (Boltzmann, through χℓ\chi_\ellχℓ​) of Lecture 2. Its shape is the Voigt profile — mathematically the real part of the Faddeeva function, reproduced here through Kurucz's Harris-table approximation — convolving a thermal-plus-turbulent Doppler core with natural, Stark, and van der Waals wings. You overlaid the result on the H−^-− continuum and saw the opacity spike that becomes an absorption line.

One line is a demonstration; a real spectrum is a forest. Lecture 5 reads the Kurucz line list — over a million atomic transitions — assigns each its log⁡gf\log gfloggf, excitation potential, and damping constants, and sums their Voigt profiles onto a wavelength grid to build the total line opacity at every depth, ready for the radiative transfer that turns it into the spectrum.
