A stellar spectrum is not just a curve of brightness against wavelength. It is the record of photons being created, absorbed, scattered, and finally released from the outer layers of a star. The same spectrum contains several kinds of information at once. Its broad continuum scale is mainly thermal. Its absorption lines remember which atoms and ions are present, how their energy levels are populated, and how pressure and velocity fields shape the escaping radiation.

The theoretical task is to predict that spectrum from physical inputs. A synthetic spectrum is the forward prediction: given a star's effective temperature TeffT_{\rm eff}Teff​, surface gravity ggg, and chemical composition, what flux should emerge at each wavelength?

The first object to build is not a line list or a transfer solver. It is an atmosphere: a run of temperature, pressure, and depth through the photosphere. The version built here is deliberately simple, but it already has the pieces every stellar-atmosphere calculation needs: a thermal radiation scale, a depth coordinate tied to photon escape, a temperature structure, and a pressure scale.

The Forward Problem

An observed stellar spectrum gives the flux received as a function of wavelength. Reading physical parameters from that spectrum is an inverse problem. The more controlled problem is the forward one:

(Teff, log⁡g, composition)⟶Fλ.(T_{\rm eff},\ \log g,\ \mathrm{composition}) \quad\longrightarrow\quad F_\lambda .(Teff​, logg, composition)⟶Fλ​.

Here FλF_\lambdaFλ​ is the flux emerging from the stellar surface per unit wavelength. Flux is energy per unit area per unit time per wavelength interval, integrated over the outward directions. It is what a distant observer receives after the usual geometric dilution by distance.

The first input, TeffT_{\rm eff}Teff​, is the effective temperature. It is defined by the total emergent flux:

F=σTeff4,F = \sigma T_{\rm eff}^4,F=σTeff4​,

where σ\sigmaσ is the Stefan--Boltzmann constant. Thus TeffT_{\rm eff}Teff​ is the temperature of a blackbody that emits the same total flux as the star. It is not the temperature at every depth. A photosphere has a temperature gradient: deeper layers are hotter, and photons at different wavelengths can escape from different depths.

The second input is log⁡g\log glogg, the base-10 logarithm of the surface gravity in CGS units. Surface gravity is the downward acceleration felt by material near the stellar surface. A solar value log⁡g=4.44\log g=4.44logg=4.44 means

g≈104.44 cm s−2.g \approx 10^{4.44}\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}} .g≈104.44 cms−2.

Gravity matters because gas pressure must support the weight of the material above each layer. At fixed temperature, a high-gravity atmosphere is more compressed than a low-gravity atmosphere.

The composition matters because atoms, ions, molecules, and free electrons determine the opacity. Opacity is what decides which photons can escape and which photons are absorbed or scattered before leaving the star.

It is useful to separate the calculation into two physical objects:

  1. A model atmosphere gives temperature, pressure, density, and related thermodynamic quantities as functions of depth.
  2. Spectral synthesis uses that atmosphere to compute wavelength-dependent opacity and solve how radiation escapes through it.

The model atmosphere is the physical stage on which the spectrum is formed. Without it, the phrase "a line forms higher than the continuum" has no meaning, because there is no depth scale and no temperature or pressure assigned to that depth.

The atmosphere below uses four simplifying assumptions.

Static means there is no time dependence and no bulk acceleration. The gas is allowed to have pressure and gravity, but it is not expanding, collapsing, or pulsating.

Plane-parallel means a small patch of the photosphere is treated as a flat slab. This is appropriate when the thickness of the line-forming layers is tiny compared with the stellar radius.

Grey means the opacity is treated as independent of wavelength. Real opacities vary strongly with wavelength; the grey approximation is a controlled way to get a first temperature-pressure structure before detailed line opacity is introduced.

Local thermodynamic equilibrium (LTE) means the material at each depth is described by a local temperature. The radiation field can stream outward and need not be a blackbody everywhere, but the matter locally has thermal level populations and thermal emission set by TTT.

Thermal Radiation: The Planck Function

A stellar atmosphere is not a solid surface with a single temperature. Radiation is emitted and absorbed throughout a layer of gas. The first local quantity we need is therefore the radiation produced by matter at one temperature.

A gas in thermodynamic equilibrium at temperature TTT emits a blackbody spectrum. The relevant quantity is specific intensity, usually written IνI_\nuIν​ or IλI_\lambdaIλ​. Specific intensity measures radiation per unit area, per unit time, per unit frequency or wavelength interval, per unit solid angle. It keeps track of direction, which later matters because photons moving vertically and photons moving at a slant pass through different amounts of material.

The blackbody specific intensity per unit frequency is the Planck function:

Bν(T)=2hν3c2 1ehν/kT−1[erg s−1 cm−2 Hz−1 sr−1].B_\nu(T) = \frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\, \frac{1}{e^{h\nu/kT}-1} \qquad [\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}\,Hz^{-1}\,sr^{-1}}].Bν​(T)=c22hν3​ehν/kT−11​[ergs−1cm−2Hz−1sr−1].

The factors have simple meanings. The 2ν2/c22\nu^2/c^22ν2/c2 part counts electromagnetic modes per frequency interval and solid angle. The extra factor hνh\nuhν is the energy per photon. The denominator ehν/kT−1e^{h\nu/kT}-1ehν/kT−1 is the Bose-Einstein occupation factor for photons in thermal equilibrium. The dimensionless ratio

x≡hνkTx \equiv \frac{h\nu}{kT}x≡kThν​

compares photon energy to thermal energy. When xxx is small, many photons occupy the mode. When xxx is large, the mode is exponentially hard to populate.

Spectra are often plotted against wavelength. The wavelength form is not obtained by simply replacing ν\nuν with c/λc/\lambdac/λ, because a frequency bin and a wavelength bin have different widths. Conservation of energy in the bin requires

Bλ ∣dλ∣=Bν ∣dν∣.B_\lambda\,|d\lambda| = B_\nu\,|d\nu|.Bλ​∣dλ∣=Bν​∣dν∣.

Since ν=c/λ\nu=c/\lambdaν=c/λ, we have ∣dν/dλ∣=c/λ2|d\nu/d\lambda|=c/\lambda^2∣dν/dλ∣=c/λ2, and therefore

Bλ(T)=Bν(T)cλ2=2hc2λ5 1ehc/λkT−1.B_\lambda(T) = B_\nu(T)\frac{c}{\lambda^2} = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\, \frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda kT}-1}.Bλ​(T)=Bν​(T)λ2c​=λ52hc2​ehc/λkT−11​.

Here λ\lambdaλ is measured in centimetres if CGS units are used. This distinction between BνB_\nuBν​ and BλB_\lambdaBλ​ is a common source of mistakes: they describe the same radiation, but per different interval.

Two limits are worth keeping in mind. In the Rayleigh--Jeans limit, hν≪kTh\nu\ll kThν≪kT, the exponential can be expanded and

Bν≈2ν2kTc2.B_\nu \approx \frac{2\nu^2 kT}{c^2}.Bν​≈c22ν2kT​.

In the Wien limit, hν≫kTh\nu\gg kThν≫kT,

Bν≈2hν3c2e−hν/kT.B_\nu \approx \frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}e^{-h\nu/kT}.Bν​≈c22hν3​e−hν/kT.

Optical photons in a solar photosphere lie between these limits. The energy scale is high enough that the intensity changes rapidly with temperature, which is one reason optical spectra are powerful temperature diagnostics.

For numerical work it is better to avoid the literal denominator ehν/kT−1e^{h\nu/kT}-1ehν/kT−1, because exe^xex can become very large in the Wien tail. The physics is unchanged; only the algebraic form changes. With

1ex−1=e−x1−e−x,\frac{1}{e^x-1} = \frac{e^{-x}}{1-e^{-x}},ex−11​=1−e−xe−x​,

Bν=[2hc2(1015 Hz)3] ν153 e−x1−e−x.B_\nu = \left[\frac{2h}{c^2}(10^{15}\,\mathrm{Hz})^3\right]\, \nu_{15}^3\, \frac{e^{-x}}{1-e^{-x}}.Bν​=[c22h​(1015Hz)3]ν153​1−e−xe−x​.

def planck_nu(frequency_hz, temperature_K):
    """Planck B_nu(T) in CGS [erg s^-1 cm^-2 Hz^-1 sr^-1].

    frequency_hz : photon frequency nu [Hz]
    temperature_K: gas temperature T   [K]
    """
    frequency = to_device(frequency_hz)
    temperature = to_device(temperature_K)

    exponent = planck_constant * frequency / (boltzmann_constant * temperature)
    exp_minus_exponent = torch.exp(-exponent)
    occupation_factor = exp_minus_exponent / (1.0 - exp_minus_exponent)

    scaled_frequency = frequency / frequency_scale_hz
    return planck_nu_prefactor * scaled_frequency**3 * occupation_factor

Optical Depth and the Photosphere

The Planck function tells us what thermal radiation matter would emit locally at temperature TTT. It does not tell us whether that radiation reaches space. A photon created deep in a star can be absorbed or scattered many times before it escapes, while a photon created near the top may leave almost immediately.

This means the natural depth coordinate is not geometric height by itself. A photon does not mainly care how many kilometres it travels. It cares how many chances it has to interact with matter before reaching free space. If the opacity is large, a physically thin layer can be opaque. If the opacity is small, a physically thick layer can be nearly transparent.

The mass extinction coefficient κλ\kappa_\lambdaκλ​ has units cm2 g−1\mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}cm2g−1. It is the effective blocking area per gram of material at wavelength λ\lambdaλ. The subscript λ\lambdaλ is a reminder that real opacity depends on wavelength. In this first atmosphere, κλ\kappa_\lambdaκλ​ is treated as a material property: it tells us how strongly one gram of gas removes light from a beam. Computing κλ\kappa_\lambdaκλ​ from atoms, ions, molecules, free electrons, and spectral lines is a separate physics problem; here we only need what opacity does once it is known.

Multiplying by the mass density ρ\rhoρ gives κλρ\kappa_\lambda\rhoκλ​ρ in cm−1\mathrm{cm^{-1}}cm−1, an extinction probability per unit length. Its inverse is the photon mean free path,

ℓλ=1κλρ.\ell_\lambda = \frac{1}{\kappa_\lambda\rho}.ℓλ​=κλ​ρ1​.

Optical depth is distance measured in mean-free-path units. A change dτλ=1d\tau_\lambda=1dτλ​=1 means roughly one interaction length. Let zzz increase outward. Looking vertically through the atmosphere,

dτλ=−κλρ dz.d\tau_\lambda = -\kappa_\lambda \rho\,dz .dτλ​=−κλ​ρdz.

Here extinction means removal of photons from a beam by absorption plus scattering. The minus sign says that τλ\tau_\lambdaτλ​ increases inward: the deeper the photon starts, the more material lies above it. A photon born at τλ≫1\tau_\lambda\gg1τλ​≫1 is many mean free paths from escape. A photon born at τλ≪1\tau_\lambda\ll1τλ​≪1 is already in optically thin material.

The exponential attenuation law follows directly from this definition. Along a short path segment, the fractional loss of beam intensity is proportional to the optical-depth step:

dIλ=−Iλ dτλ.dI_\lambda = -I_\lambda\,d\tau_\lambda .dIλ​=−Iλ​dτλ​.

Dividing by IλI_\lambdaIλ​ and integrating from τ=0\tau=0τ=0 to τ\tauτ gives

∫I0IdIλIλ=−∫0τdτλ,⇒ln⁡II0=−τ.\int_{I_0}^{I}\frac{dI_\lambda}{I_\lambda} = -\int_0^\tau d\tau_\lambda, \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \ln\frac{I}{I_0}=-\tau .∫I0​I​Iλ​dIλ​​=−∫0τ​dτλ​,⇒lnI0​I​=−τ.

Therefore a beam that only loses photons and gains no new emission has

I=I0e−τ.I = I_0 e^{-\tau}.I=I0​e−τ.

This attenuation law gives the practical meaning of optical depth: τ≪1\tau\ll1τ≪1 is transparent, τ∼1\tau\sim1τ∼1 is the transition between transparent and opaque, and τ≫1\tau\gg1τ≫1 is opaque.

There is also a more precise reason that optical depth of order unity appears in photosphere arguments. When material emits as well as absorbs, the emergent intensity is a weighted average of local emission along the ray. Material at very large optical depth is exponentially hidden, while material at very small optical depth contributes little because there is not much of it. For a ray with direction cosine μ=cos⁡θ\mu=\cos\thetaμ=cosθ, the main contribution comes from τλ∼μ\tau_\lambda\sim\muτλ​∼μ.

The observed flux is an angular average over outward rays, so the precise numerical depth depends on the angular boundary model. The robust physical point is that the photosphere is located around optical depth unity, not at a geometric surface. Different wavelengths and line strengths sample different depths because κλ\kappa_\lambdaκλ​ changes with wavelength. A strong line core has large opacity, reaches τλ∼1\tau_\lambda\sim1τλ​∼1 higher in the atmosphere, and therefore samples cooler gas. A weak line wing or continuum point reaches τλ∼1\tau_\lambda\sim1τλ​∼1 deeper down.

Local Thermodynamic Equilibrium

Optical depth tells us how hard it is for radiation to escape. We also need to know what the gas contributes to the radiation field while a beam passes through it.

The source function SλS_\lambdaSλ​ is the local emission divided by the local extinction, with the same units as specific intensity. It is the intensity the material would like to impose on the radiation field locally. If the incoming intensity is smaller than SλS_\lambdaSλ​, local emission tends to brighten the beam. If the incoming intensity is larger than SλS_\lambdaSλ​, extinction tends to dim it.

In optical-depth form, the radiative-transfer equation along a ray is

μdIλdτλ=Iλ−Sλ,\mu\frac{dI_\lambda}{d\tau_\lambda} = I_\lambda-S_\lambda,μdτλ​dIλ​​=Iλ​−Sλ​,

where μ=cos⁡θ\mu=\cos\thetaμ=cosθ is the direction cosine relative to the outward normal. The attenuation law came from the special case with no source term. Stellar spectra require the full balance: extinction removes photons from a beam, while the source function adds locally emitted photons.

The simplifying assumption is local thermodynamic equilibrium, or LTE. LTE means that, at each depth, collisions are frequent enough for the material state to be described by a local temperature TTT. The radiation field may stream outward and need not be a blackbody everywhere, but the matter locally behaves as if it were in thermodynamic equilibrium.

Kirchhoff's law says that, in thermodynamic equilibrium, material that absorbs efficiently at a wavelength must also emit efficiently at that wavelength. If this were not true, matter in equilibrium would radiatively heat up or cool down on its own, contradicting equilibrium.

Applied locally in LTE, Kirchhoff's law makes the thermal source function equal to the Planck function at the local temperature:

Sλ=Bλ(T).S_\lambda = B_\lambda(T).Sλ​=Bλ​(T).

Here true absorption means photons are converted into internal energy of the gas, followed by thermal re-emission. Scattering is different: it redirects photons and can have its own source term. For this first atmosphere, the essential LTE statement is simple: once we know TTT at a depth, we know the local thermal emission scale there.

A Grey Model Atmosphere

A model atmosphere assigns physical conditions to depth. For a first calculation, the central quantity is the temperature structure T(τ)T(\tau)T(τ), because LTE makes the thermal source function equal to Bλ[T(τ)]B_\lambda[T(\tau)]Bλ​[T(τ)]. Once TTT is known, hydrostatic equilibrium supplies a corresponding pressure scale.

The simplest useful atmosphere is a grey atmosphere. Grey means that the opacity is treated as independent of wavelength. Then there is one optical-depth coordinate τ\tauτ rather than a different τλ\tau_\lambdaτλ​ at every wavelength. This approximation cannot produce absorption lines, because lines require wavelength-dependent opacity. Its purpose is narrower and important: it gives a continuum pressure-temperature scaffold from only TeffT_{\rm eff}Teff​ and log⁡g\log glogg.

The atmosphere is in radiative equilibrium. This means each layer passes along the same total radiative flux. A layer may absorb and emit radiation, but after all wavelengths and directions are counted it has no net radiative heating or cooling. The outward flux is fixed by the effective temperature:

F=σTeff4.F = \sigma T_{\rm eff}^4.F=σTeff4​.

The grey temperature law can be understood without solving the full angle-dependent transfer problem. The useful idea is to compress the radiation field into angular moments. This uses the same specific intensity introduced with the Planck function, but now integrated over all frequencies and written only as a function of ray direction: I(μ)I(\mu)I(μ). This frequency-integrated intensity is called bolometric intensity. In a plane-parallel atmosphere let μ=cos⁡θ\mu=\cos\thetaμ=cosθ, where θ\thetaθ is measured from the outward normal. The three lowest moments are

J=12∫−11I(μ) dμ,H=12∫−11μI(μ) dμ,K=12∫−11μ2I(μ) dμ.J=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} I(\mu)\,d\mu, \qquad H=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} \mu I(\mu)\,d\mu, \qquad K=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1} \mu^2 I(\mu)\,d\mu.J=21​∫−11​I(μ)dμ,H=21​∫−11​μI(μ)dμ,K=21​∫−11​μ2I(μ)dμ.

Here JJJ is the angle-averaged intensity, HHH is the flux moment, and KKK is the radiation-pressure moment. The physical flux is

F=4πH.F=4\pi H.F=4πH.

In grey LTE, radiative equilibrium connects the local radiation field to the local thermal emission:

J=B(T)=σπT4.J = B(T)=\frac{\sigma}{\pi}T^4.J=B(T)=πσ​T4.

The total flux is constant with depth and is fixed by the effective temperature, so

H=F4π=σTeff44π.H=\frac{F}{4\pi}=\frac{\sigma T_{\rm eff}^4}{4\pi}.H=4πF​=4πσTeff4​​.

One more relation is needed because the moments contain JJJ, HHH, and KKK. A closure is an approximation that relates the higher moment to the lower ones so the system can be solved. The Eddington approximation uses

K≃J3.K\simeq \frac{J}{3}.K≃3J​.

This relation is exact for an isotropic radiation field, because the angular average of μ2\mu^2μ2 is 1/31/31/3. It is not exact at the surface, where radiation streams preferentially outward, but it is the simplest useful closure for a grey atmosphere.

The moment equation for the flux then gives the compact result

13dJdτ=H.\frac{1}{3}\frac{dJ}{d\tau}=H.31​dτdJ​=H.

Substituting J=(σ/π)T4J=(\sigma/\pi)T^4J=(σ/π)T4 and H=σTeff4/(4π)H=\sigma T_{\rm eff}^4/(4\pi)H=σTeff4​/(4π) gives

dT4dτ=34Teff4.\frac{dT^4}{d\tau}=\frac{3}{4}T_{\rm eff}^4.dτdT4​=43​Teff4​.

This is the important physical result: in a grey radiative-equilibrium atmosphere, T4T^4T4 increases linearly with optical depth. Integrating gives

T4(τ)=34Teff4(τ+C),T^4(\tau) = \frac{3}{4}T_{\rm eff}^4(\tau+C),T4(τ)=43​Teff4​(τ+C),

where CCC is a boundary constant. The slope is set by radiative equilibrium; the additive constant is set by the surface boundary condition. The simplest Eddington boundary gives C=2/3C=2/3C=2/3, so

T4(τ)=34Teff4(τ+23).T^4(\tau) = \frac{3}{4}T_{\rm eff}^4\left(\tau+\frac{2}{3}\right).T4(τ)=43​Teff4​(τ+32​).

This makes T(τ=2/3)=TeffT(\tau=2/3)=T_{\rm eff}T(τ=2/3)=Teff​. That is the origin of the familiar statement that the visible photosphere is near optical depth 2/32/32/3. It is not a material surface. It is the depth where the emergent continuum samples gas with roughly the effective temperature.

More detailed grey surface solutions replace the constant CCC by a dimensionless Hopf function q(τ)q(\tau)q(τ):

T4(τ)=34Teff4[τ+q(τ)].T^4(\tau) = \frac{3}{4}T_{\rm eff}^4[\tau+q(\tau)].T4(τ)=43​Teff4​[τ+q(τ)].

The Hopf function is not an extra stellar parameter. It is a compact way to encode the angular boundary correction near the surface while leaving the deeper radiative-equilibrium gradient intact. In deep layers q(τ)q(\tau)q(τ) approaches an almost constant offset, so the temperature gradient remains the one derived above.

The moment derivation fixes the slope of T4T^4T4 with optical depth, but it does not force one particular surface correction. The constant q=2/3q=2/3q=2/3 is the simplest Eddington boundary choice. The numerical atmosphere below instead uses the smooth Kurucz/ATLAS grey-start fit

τ+q(τ)=0.710+τ−0.1331e−3.4488τ.\tau + q(\tau) = 0.710+\tau-0.1331e^{-3.4488\tau}.τ+q(τ)=0.710+τ−0.1331e−3.4488τ.

Substituting this bracket into the grey law gives

T(τ)=Teff [34(0.710+τ−0.1331e−3.4488τ)]1/4.T(\tau) = T_{\rm eff}\, \left[ \frac{3}{4} \left(0.710+\tau-0.1331e^{-3.4488\tau}\right) \right]^{1/4}.T(τ)=Teff​[43​(0.710+τ−0.1331e−3.4488τ)]1/4.

This is a modeling choice made for the grey starting atmosphere, not a new physical law. The exponential term changes only the surface layers: q(0)=0.710−0.1331=0.5769q(0)=0.710-0.1331=0.5769q(0)=0.710−0.1331=0.5769, so the top boundary is slightly cooler than the constant-qqq Eddington form, while deep in the atmosphere the exponential dies away and q(τ)q(\tau)q(τ) approaches 0.7100.7100.710. The result keeps T(τ=2/3)T(\tau=2/3)T(τ=2/3) very close to TeffT_{\rm eff}Teff​ while giving a smoother surface behavior than a constant qqq.

We now need to turn the temperature law into a table of layers. The variable in the temperature law is an optical depth, so the grid should be spaced in optical depth rather than in geometric height.

Rosseland optical depth. This is not a new kind of optical depth. It is the same construction as dτλ=κλ dmd\tau_\lambda=\kappa_\lambda\,dmdτλ​=κλ​dm, but with the many wavelength-dependent opacities replaced by one representative opacity. In a real atmosphere, blue continuum, red continuum, weak line wings, and strong line cores all see different κλ\kappa_\lambdaκλ​. A model atmosphere still needs one common depth coordinate on which to tabulate TTT, pressure, and column mass. The standard choice is the Rosseland optical depth τRoss\tau_{\rm Ross}τRoss​, defined by

dτRoss=κRoss dm,d\tau_{\rm Ross}=\kappa_{\rm Ross}\,dm,dτRoss​=κRoss​dm,

where mmm is column mass and κRoss\kappa_{\rm Ross}κRoss​ is the Rosseland mean opacity. The only change from the earlier wavelength-by-wavelength definition is the replacement

κλ⟶κRoss.\kappa_\lambda \longrightarrow \kappa_{\rm Ross}.κλ​⟶κRoss​.

Why this particular mean? Deep in an optically thick atmosphere, radiation escapes by diffusion. In that limit, the wavelengths that carry energy most efficiently are the relatively transparent windows, not the most opaque wavelengths. The Rosseland mean captures that by averaging the inverse opacity:

1κRoss=∫0∞κλ−1 (∂Bλ/∂T) dλ∫0∞(∂Bλ/∂T) dλ.\frac{1}{\kappa_{\rm Ross}} = \frac{\int_0^\infty \kappa_\lambda^{-1}\,(\partial B_\lambda/\partial T)\,d\lambda} {\int_0^\infty(\partial B_\lambda/\partial T)\,d\lambda}.κRoss​1​=∫0∞​(∂Bλ​/∂T)dλ∫0∞​κλ−1​(∂Bλ​/∂T)dλ​.

The factor ∂Bλ/∂T\partial B_\lambda/\partial T∂Bλ​/∂T says which wavelengths respond most strongly to a local temperature change, and the factor κλ−1\kappa_\lambda^{-1}κλ−1​ gives extra weight to wavelengths where photons travel farther before interacting. This is why the Rosseland mean is a harmonic, diffusion-weighted opacity, not a simple arithmetic average.

Why it is convenient for spectra. A spectrum calculation eventually computes a different optical depth τλ\tau_\lambdaτλ​ at each wavelength. The atmosphere itself, however, is stored as one table of physical conditions. τRoss\tau_{\rm Ross}τRoss​ provides that table's depth coordinate: it orders layers by how deeply they sit in the diffusion problem, while still letting each wavelength form at its own τλ∼1\tau_\lambda\sim1τλ​∼1 surface during spectral synthesis.

In the strictly grey atmosphere built here, κλ\kappa_\lambdaκλ​ is independent of wavelength, so κRoss\kappa_{\rm Ross}κRoss​ reduces to that same grey opacity and τRoss\tau_{\rm Ross}τRoss​ is just the single optical-depth coordinate τ\tauτ used in the derivation above. We keep the Rosseland name because it is the same depth coordinate used once the opacity is allowed to vary with wavelength.

For the solar example, evaluate T(τRoss)T(\tau_{\rm Ross})T(τRoss​) on an 80-layer logarithmic grid. The grid runs from optically thin surface layers to optically thick diffusion layers. Logarithmic spacing is convenient because the photosphere occupies a narrow region around τRoss∼1\tau_{\rm Ross}\sim1τRoss​∼1, while the useful atmosphere spans many orders of magnitude in optical depth.

def grey_temperature(teff_K, optical_depth):
    """Grey-atmosphere temperature T(tau) using the Kurucz/ATLAS Hopf fit."""
    effective_temperature = to_device(teff_K)
    tau_value = to_device(optical_depth)
    hopf_bracket = 0.710 + tau_value - 0.1331 * torch.exp(-3.4488 * tau_value)
    return effective_temperature * (0.75 * hopf_bracket)**0.25

Hydrostatic Equilibrium: Pressure and Column Mass

Temperature alone is not an atmosphere. We also need pressure, because pressure sets density and controls many line-broadening and ionization effects.

Pressure is force per unit area. In a static atmosphere, the upward pressure force on a layer balances the downward weight of material above it. In a plane-parallel slab this balance is one-dimensional:

dPtotaldz=−ρg,\frac{dP_{\rm total}}{dz} = -\rho g ,dzdPtotal​​=−ρg,

where PtotalP_{\rm total}Ptotal​ is gas plus radiation pressure, ρ\rhoρ is mass density, and ggg is surface gravity. Column mass mmm is the mass above one square centimetre of the stellar surface. In terms of mmm, hydrostatic equilibrium becomes especially simple:

dPtotaldm=g,⇒Ptotal=gm\frac{dP_{\rm total}}{dm} = g, \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad P_{\rm total}=gmdmdPtotal​​=g,⇒Ptotal​=gm

if the pressure is measured relative to the top boundary.

Hydrostatic equilibrium on an optical-depth grid. The equation above naturally gives pressure as a function of column mass. The temperature law, however, is written as a function of Rosseland optical depth. Reusing the Rosseland relation defined above,

dτRoss=κRoss dm.d\tau_{\rm Ross}=\kappa_{\rm Ross}\,dm .dτRoss​=κRoss​dm.

Combining this with dPtotal/dm=gdP_{\rm total}/dm=gdPtotal​/dm=g gives

dPtotaldτRoss=gκRoss.\frac{dP_{\rm total}}{d\tau_{\rm Ross}} = \frac{g}{\kappa_{\rm Ross}} .dτRoss​dPtotal​​=κRoss​g​.

This equation explains why opacity matters for pressure at a given optical depth. If κRoss\kappa_{\rm Ross}κRoss​ is large, little mass is needed to reach τRoss∼1\tau_{\rm Ross}\sim1τRoss​∼1, so the pressure there is smaller. If κRoss\kappa_{\rm Ross}κRoss​ is small, more mass lies above the same optical depth, so the pressure is larger.

Why use κRoss=1\kappa_{\rm Ross}=1κRoss​=1 here? This first grey atmosphere has not yet computed a physical opacity table. The grey temperature law supplies TTT as a function of optical depth, but it does not by itself say how many grams per square centimetre correspond to that optical depth. To attach a first pressure scale, choose a constant unit opacity,

κRoss=1 cm2 g−1.\kappa_{\rm Ross}=1\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}.κRoss​=1 cm2g−1.

This is a normalization for the first model, not a claim that the Sun's Rosseland opacity is really 1 cm2 g−11\ \mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}1 cm2g−1. If a different constant opacity κ0\kappa_0κ0​ were chosen, the same temperature law would give

m=τRossκ0,Ptotal=gτRossκ0.m=\frac{\tau_{\rm Ross}}{\kappa_0}, \qquad P_{\rm total}=\frac{g\tau_{\rm Ross}}{\kappa_0}.m=κ0​τRoss​​,Ptotal​=κ0​gτRoss​​.

With the unit choice κ0=1\kappa_0=1κ0​=1, this reduces numerically to

m=τRoss,Ptotal=gτRoss.m=\tau_{\rm Ross}, \qquad P_{\rm total}=g\tau_{\rm Ross}.m=τRoss​,Ptotal​=gτRoss​.

The total pressure contains gas pressure plus radiation pressure. Radiation pressure is the momentum flux carried by an isotropic radiation field:

Prad=4σ3cT4.P_{\rm rad}=\frac{4\sigma}{3c}T^4.Prad​=3c4σ​T4.

In a solar photosphere this correction is small compared with gas pressure. Since the hydrostatic pressure above was measured relative to the top boundary, the radiation-pressure correction is also measured relative to its top value:

ΔPrad=4σ3c[T4(τ)−T4(τtop)],Pgas=Ptotal−ΔPrad.\Delta P_{\rm rad} = \frac{4\sigma}{3c}\left[T^4(\tau)-T^4(\tau_{\rm top})\right], \qquad P_{\rm gas}=P_{\rm total}-\Delta P_{\rm rad}.ΔPrad​=3c4σ​[T4(τ)−T4(τtop​)],Pgas​=Ptotal​−ΔPrad​.

A local mass density would require an equation of state. For this first atmosphere, pressure and column mass are enough to define the hydrostatic scale.

What Has Been Built

Starting from Teff=5770 KT_{\rm eff}=5770\,\mathrm{K}Teff​=5770K and log⁡g=4.44\log g=4.44logg=4.44, we have built a self-contained first atmosphere. The Planck function supplies the thermal radiation scale. Optical depth supplies the natural depth coordinate for photon escape. LTE connects the local temperature to the thermal source function. The grey temperature relation supplies T(τRoss)T(\tau_{\rm Ross})T(τRoss​). Hydrostatic equilibrium supplies the first pressure and column-mass scale after we choose the unit-opacity normalization κRoss=1\kappa_{\rm Ross}=1κRoss​=1.

The resulting atmosphere is a table of layers. Each layer has an optical depth, a temperature, a gas pressure, and a column mass. Reading downward in the table means moving inward through the photosphere: photons have farther to travel before escape, the material is hotter, and the pressure is larger because more mass lies above the layer.

This is not yet a detailed stellar spectrum. It has no wavelength-dependent opacity, no line opacity, no electron-density solution, and no radiative-transfer integral through a line-forming atmosphere. It does have the essential physical scaffold: a temperature structure on a Rosseland-depth grid and a first pressure scale tied to an explicit opacity normalization.
