Introduction: building the atmosphere we have been given

For eight lectures the model atmosphere has been a given. We took its run of temperature, gas pressure, and density with depth — the columns of a .atm file — and on top of it built the equation of state, the continuous and line opacities, and the radiative-transfer solver, until we reproduced the solar spectrum to machine precision. But where did that atmosphere come from? In this static, plane-parallel setup the two structural equilibrium constraints are hydrostatic equilibrium, that the gas neither collapses under its own weight nor blows away, and radiative equilibrium, that the energy carried outward by radiation is conserved at every depth; closing the model also requires an equation of state, opacities, a composition, boundary conditions, and a convection treatment. This lecture builds the first structural constraint. It is the start of the inverse half of the course: instead of taking the structure and computing the spectrum, we compute the structure itself from the two numbers that fix the grey cold start of a star — its effective temperature TeffT_{\rm eff}Teff​ and its surface gravity log⁡g\log glogg. (A full model also fixes the composition and microturbulence; in the simplified grey setup here, TeffT_{\rm eff}Teff​ and log⁡g\log glogg are enough.)

Lecture 1 already built most of the pieces we need here, and we will lean on them rather than re-derive them. It wrote down the grey/Hopf temperature law T(τ)T(\tau)T(τ), laid out the 80-layer optical-depth grid, and introduced hydrostatic equilibrium in optical depth, dP/dτ=g/κdP/d\tau = g/\kappadP/dτ=g/κ. It also took one shortcut: on the cold start it set κ≡1\kappa\equiv1κ≡1, integrated the balance by hand to the one-line result Ptotal=gτP_{\rm total}=g\tauPtotal​=gτ, and found it agreed with the reference only to about one part in 10510^{5}105.

That one part in 10510^{5}105 is worth being precise about, because it is not a flaw in the closed form. For κ≡1\kappa\equiv1κ≡1 and a zero-pressure top boundary, Ptotal=gτP_{\rm total}=g\tauPtotal​=gτ is the exact analytic solution of the hydrostatic equation, not an approximation to it. The residual comes from the things the one-liner left out: the production code's boundary seed, its finite-grid predictor-corrector, its radiation-pressure increment and subtraction, and the fact that the reference array is a gas pressure while gτg\taugτ is a total pressure. Reproducing that stored gas pressure to the last bit is the one genuinely new thing this lecture does. We recap the temperature and the grid quickly, then spend our effort where Lecture 1 stopped: on the same boundary convention, the same radiation-pressure treatment, and the exact predictor-corrector that integrates the equation in log pressure. Hydrostatic equilibrium, in the form the codes integrate (Lecture 1)

The first lecture introduced hydrostatic equilibrium — the balance between the pressure gradient and the weight of the overlying gas — and rewrote it in optical depth τ\tauτ, the variable the radiation field cares about. With height zzz measured upward, τ\tauτ and column mass increasing inward, gravity ggg constant across the thin photosphere, and κ\kappaκ a Rosseland mass opacity in cm2 g−1\mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}cm2g−1, the plane-parallel hydrostatic equation becomes

dPdτ=gκ.\frac{dP}{d\tau} = \frac{g}{\kappa}.dτdP​=κg​.

We take that as our starting point. Read it as a statement about the column mass: since dP/dm=gdP/d m = gdP/dm=g, integrating it gives the weight per unit area of the overlying gas, g mg\,mgm. With the usual zero-pressure top boundary this is just Ptotal=g mP_{\rm total} = g\,mPtotal​=gm — up to the chosen top-boundary constant, which is exactly the surface radiation pressure Prad(0)P_{\rm rad}(0)Prad​(0) when the boundary carries no gas, and which we will see the integrator handle explicitly later. Here mmm is the column mass, the grams of material above a square centimetre; Kurucz calls this quantity RHOX, and it is a single compound symbol m=∫z∞ρ dz′m=\int_z^\infty\rho\,dz'm=∫z∞​ρdz′, not the local density ρ\rhoρ multiplied by a depth coordinate xxx. The optical-depth form makes the opacity explicit: where the gas is more opaque (large κ\kappaκ), a given range of τ\tauτ spans less pressure, because you reach optical depth one in less material. In this atmosphere-construction context, τ\tauτ means Rosseland optical depth and κ\kappaκ the Rosseland-mean opacity, unless stated otherwise.

So far this is all Lecture 1. What that lecture did not do is integrate the equation on the production grid: it set κ≡1\kappa\equiv1κ≡1 (i.e. κRoss=1 cm2 g−1\kappa_{\rm Ross}=1\,\mathrm{cm^2\,g^{-1}}κRoss​=1cm2g−1 in the code's cgs convention) and read off the analytic Ptotal=gτP_{\rm total}=g\tauPtotal​=gτ in one line. The rest of this lecture reproduces the discrete integration the reference uses. To do it we need two inputs at every depth — the temperature (which sets PradP_{\rm rad}Prad​ and, through the equation of state, the density) and the opacity κ(τ)\kappa(\tau)κ(τ) — so we recap those two first, then build the integrator.

Recap (Lecture 1): the grey/Hopf temperature

The temperature structure is the input to the pressure integration. Lecture 1 derived it in full from the grey transfer equation; here we only need the result, so this is a one-paragraph recap. The grey/Hopf law is T(τ)=Teff [34(0.710+τ−0.1331 e−3.4488τ)]1/4T(\tau) = T_{\rm eff}\,[\tfrac34(0.710 + \tau - 0.1331\,e^{-3.4488\tau})]^{1/4}T(τ)=Teff​[43​(0.710+τ−0.1331e−3.4488τ)]1/4 — Kurucz's analytic (exponential) fit to the Hopf function q(τ)q(\tau)q(τ), with qqq rising from 1/3≈0.5771/\sqrt3\approx0.5771/3​≈0.577 at the surface to 0.7100.7100.710 deep down (see Lecture 1 for the derivation and the usual τ∼2/3\tau\sim2/3τ∼2/3 photospheric scale). The code below is the identical fit from Lecture 1: it evaluates the bracket 34(τ+q)\tfrac34(\tau+q)43​(τ+q), floors it at 10−30010^{-300}10−300 so the fourth root can never see a negative argument (a safety guard that never triggers for a real atmosphere), and takes the 1/41/41/4 power. We re-include it here because it feeds the pressure integration that follows.

[2]Python 
def grey_temperature(teff, tau):
    """Eddington-Kurucz grey T(tau): Kurucz's analytic fit to the Hopf function q(tau)."""
    # the bracket (3/4)(tau + q(tau)), with q(tau) the exponential Hopf fit
    bracket = 0.75 * (0.710 + tau - 0.1331 * np.exp(-3.4488 * tau))

    # T = Teff * bracket^(1/4); the floor keeps the fourth root off a negative argument
    return float(teff) * np.power(np.maximum(bracket, 1e-300), 0.25)

def check(name, got, ref):
    """Report max|relative diff| between a from-scratch array and the reference; flag bit-exact."""
    got, ref = np.asarray(got, float), np.asarray(ref, float)

    # mask out exact zeros, where a relative difference is undefined
    m = np.abs(ref) > 0
    rel = np.zeros_like(ref)
    rel[m] = np.abs(got[m] - ref[m]) / np.abs(ref[m])

    tag = "bit-exact" if np.array_equal(got, ref) else f"max|rel| = {rel.max():.2e}"
    print(f"  {name:12s}  {tag}")
    return rel.max()

Radiation pressure

This is new content — Lecture 1 mentioned PradP_{\rm rad}Prad​ in passing but the integrator here actually carries it, so it is worth getting the definition exactly right. The total pressure has two parts, gas and radiation: Ptotal=Pgas+PradP_{\rm total} = P_{\rm gas} + P_{\rm rad}Ptotal​=Pgas​+Prad​. The hydrostatic balance supports the total, but the equation of state and the line opacities care about the gas pressure, so we will subtract PradP_{\rm rad}Prad​ at the end.

Be careful with the coefficient. The radiation energy density of an isotropic field is aradT4a_{\rm rad}T^4arad​T4 with arad≈7.566×10−15 erg cm−3 K−4a_{\rm rad}\approx7.566\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{erg\,cm^{-3}\,K^{-4}}arad​≈7.566×10−15ergcm−3K−4, while the corresponding radiation pressure is one third of that, Prad=13aradT4=4σ3cT4P_{\rm rad}=\tfrac13 a_{\rm rad}T^4 = \tfrac{4\sigma}{3c}T^4Prad​=31​arad​T4=3c4σ​T4.

This 13aradT4\tfrac13 a_{\rm rad}T^431​arad​T4 form is the local, diffusion/Eddington-style radiation-pressure correction the code uses: it is exact for an isotropic blackbody field and a good approximation in the deep, optically thick layers, but near the surface the radiation field is forward-peaked and anisotropic, so the true angular radiation pressure need not equal 13aradT4\tfrac13 a_{\rm rad}T^431​arad​T4 there. ATLAS adopts it as the standard structural approximation throughout. Kurucz carries the pressure coefficient 13arad\tfrac13 a_{\rm rad}31​arad​ as the constant 2.521×10−152.521\times10^{-15}2.521×10−15. The code also floors the temperature inside the fourth power at Teff4/2T_{\rm eff}^4/2Teff4​/2, so PradP_{\rm rad}Prad​ never drops below a fixed fraction of its photospheric value in the cool upper layers — a stabiliser that keeps the cold start well behaved:

Prad(τ)=2.521×10−15 max⁡ ⁣(T4, 12Teff4).P_{\rm rad}(\tau) = 2.521\times10^{-15}\,\max\!\big(T^4,\ \tfrac12 T_{\rm eff}^4\big).Prad​(τ)=2.521×10−15max(T4, 21​Teff4​).

For this solar cold start the radiation-pressure correction to the hydrostatic run is small, but it is retained because the reference code retains it and because it matters in hotter, lower-gravity atmospheres. What enters the integration is the run of PradP_{\rm rad}Prad​ relative to the surface, Prad(τ)−Prad(0)P_{\rm rad}(\tau)-P_{\rm rad}(0)Prad​(τ)−Prad​(0), since only the gradient of pressure matters. The code therefore keeps two arrays: pradk is the absolute radiation pressure at each layer, while prad = pradk - pradk[0] is the depth-dependent increment relative to the top layer (so prad[0] is exactly zero by construction). The hydrostatic integration only ever needs this increment; the surface constant is absorbed into the boundary pressure. The turbulent pressure pturb is zero on the cold start.

Why the cold start uses κ≡1\kappa\equiv1κ≡1

Hydrostatic equilibrium dP/dτ=g/κdP/d\tau = g/\kappadP/dτ=g/κ needs the opacity κ(τ)\kappa(\tau)κ(τ) — but the opacity depends on the pressure and temperature through the equation of state and the photo-absorption cross-sections, which we cannot evaluate until we have the structure. This is the bootstrap problem at the heart of building an atmosphere: structure needs opacity, opacity needs structure.

The production code breaks the loop with a cold start. On the very first pass there is no opacity table yet, so the Rosseland-mean opacity is set to a placeholder of unity at every layer, κRoss≡1\kappa_{\rm Ross}\equiv1κRoss​≡1. (Internally the opacity table — ROSSTAB — is empty, and an empty table returns 1.01.01.0.) This is deliberately crude: it gives a first guess of the pressure structure that is in the right ballpark, which ATLAS then refines over many iterations, rebuilding the real Rosseland-mean opacity from the equation of state and feeding it back into the hydrostatic integration until the structure stops changing. We are reproducing that first guess — the grey model the iteration starts from — so κ≡1\kappa\equiv1κ≡1 throughout. There is one cosmetic exception: the top layer is seeded with κ=0.1\kappa=0.1κ=0.1 purely to set the boundary value of the integration, as we see next.

Integrating in log pressure, and why

Now the new work begins: the actual integration of dP/dτ=g/κdP/d\tau = g/\kappadP/dτ=g/κ down the grid. The natural move would be to step in PPP directly, but two facts make log pressure the better variable. First, the pressure spans eight or nine decades from the top of the atmosphere to the bottom, so a fixed step in PPP would be hopelessly coarse at the top and wastefully fine at the bottom, while a step in log⁡P\log PlogP resolves every decade equally. Second, the grid is uniform in log⁡τ\log\taulogτ, and on the cold start with κ\kappaκ constant the solution P=gτP=g\tauP=gτ is a power law, so log⁡P\log PlogP is linear in log⁡τ\log\taulogτ — the integrand is smoothest in log-log variables, where a low-order multistep formula is most accurate.

The chain rule is cleanest in natural logs. Let p≡ln⁡Ptotalp\equiv\ln P_{\rm total}p≡lnPtotal​ and u≡ln⁡τu\equiv\ln\tauu≡lnτ. Then, applying d/dud/dud/du through ppp, PtotalP_{\rm total}Ptotal​, and τ\tauτ in turn,

dpdu=1Ptotal dPtotaldτ dτdu=1Ptotal gκ τ=gκ τPtotal,\frac{dp}{du} = \frac{1}{P_{\rm total}}\,\frac{dP_{\rm total}}{d\tau}\,\frac{d\tau}{du} = \frac{1}{P_{\rm total}}\,\frac{g}{\kappa}\,\tau = \frac{g}{\kappa}\,\frac{\tau}{P_{\rm total}},dudp​=Ptotal​1​dτdPtotal​​dudτ​=Ptotal​1​κg​τ=κg​Ptotal​τ​,

using dPtotal/dτ=g/κdP_{\rm total}/d\tau = g/\kappadPtotal​/dτ=g/κ from hydrostatic equilibrium and dτ/du=τd\tau/du = \taudτ/du=τ (since u=ln⁡τu=\ln\tauu=lnτ). Multiplying by the constant step Δu=Δln⁡τ\Delta u = \Delta\ln\tauΔu=Δlnτ gives the quantity the code carries from layer to layer,

The predictor-corrector integrator

This is the heart of the lecture — the exact integrator that replaces Lecture 1's one-line P=gτP=g\tauP=gτ. We integrate layer by layer from the top down, carrying a short history of the last few values of p=ln⁡Pp=\ln Pp=lnP and of the derivative Δp\Delta pΔp. Each layer is done in two stages, predict then correct.

Predictor. Extrapolate pjp_jpj​ from the history. The very first layer (j=0j=0j=0) has no history, so its pressure is set directly from the seeded boundary opacity, p0=ln⁡(g τ0/κ0)p_0 = \ln(g\,\tau_0/\kappa_0)p0​=ln(gτ0​/κ0​) with κ0=0.1\kappa_0=0.1κ0​=0.1. The next three layers (j≤3j\le3j≤3) use a one-step extrapolation pj=pj−1+Δpj−1p_j = p_{j-1} + \Delta p_{j-1}pj​=pj−1​+Δpj−1​, because the history is not yet long enough for the full formula. From the fifth layer on, a four-term multistep predictor combines the value four layers back with a weighted blend of the last three derivatives,

pjpred=3 pj−4+8 Δpj−1−4 Δpj−2+8 Δpj−33.p_j^{\rm pred} = \frac{3\,p_{j-4} + 8\,\Delta p_{j-1} - 4\,\Delta p_{j-2} + 8\,\Delta p_{j-3}}{3}.pjpred​=33pj−4​+8Δpj−1​−4Δpj−2​+8Δpj−3​​.

These are the Kurucz/ATLAS multistep coefficients; we do not derive them here but transcribe them from ATLAS. They play the same role as an explicit Adams-type predictor on this smooth log-log grid, reaching back several layers because the integrand is smooth enough there that a long stencil pays off. One useful sanity check is that the predictor is exact for a linear p(ln⁡τ)p(\ln\tau)p(lnτ) — which is precisely the cold-start limit, where κ≡1\kappa\equiv1κ≡1 makes ln⁡P\ln PlnP linear in ln⁡τ\ln\taulnτ.

Corrector. With a trial pjp_jpj​ in hand, evaluate the total pressure Ptotal,j=epjP_{{\rm total},j}=e^{p_j}Ptotal,j​=epj​, subtract the radiation (and turbulent) pressure to get the gas pressure, look up the opacity (κj=1\kappa_j=1κj​=1 on the cold start), and form the derivative Δpj=(g/κj)(τj/Ptotal,j) Δln⁡τ\Delta p_j = (g/\kappa_j)(\tau_j/P_{{\rm total},j})\,\Delta\ln\tauΔpj​=(g/κj​)(τj​/Ptotal,j​)Δlnτ. A corrector formula (analogous integer weights) then produces a refined estimate pjnewp_j^{\rm new}pjnew​ from pjp_jpj​ and the freshly computed derivative, and we average the two, pj←12(pjnew+pj)p_j \leftarrow \tfrac12(p_j^{\rm new}+p_j)pj​←21​(pjnew​+pj​), iterating until the change is below 5×10−55\times10^{-5}5×10−5.

One ordering detail is what makes this reproduce the reference to the bit, and it is worth stating plainly. The code evaluates and stores PtotalP_{\rm total}Ptotal​, the gas pressure, the opacity, and the derivative from the current trial pjp_jpj​ first, and only then tests for convergence. So on the iteration where the loop decides it is done, the stored pressure is the trial value at the start of that iteration — not a further-refined corrector. (If the loop runs more than once, that trial value is itself the averaged corrector from the previous iteration; the corrector that would have nudged it once more is computed but never applied to the stored value.) Matching this "evaluate-then-check" order is the difference between agreeing to five decimals and agreeing to all of them.

We implement this in two cells. The first, ttaup, is the set-up wrapper: it allocates the output arrays (opacity, total pressure, gas pressure), forms the constant log-tau step, zeroes the rolling history, and seeds the boundary opacity at the top layer (κ0=0.1\kappa_0=0.1κ0​=0.1, unless a positive surface radiation pressure asks for a smaller value). It then hands everything to the per-layer loop.

The boundary guard if prad[0] > 0.0 deserves a word, because the routine is natively written to receive the absolute radiation pressure (where prad[0] > 0.0 and the guard sets a tighter seed). For this cold-start benchmark we deliberately pass the pre-subtracted increment array, which forces prad[0] to be exactly zero by construction — and that is the point: a zero prad[0] deactivates the guard, replicating ATLAS's behaviour of skipping the boundary correction on the very first pass and falling back to the plain κ0=0.1\kappa_0=0.1κ0​=0.1 seed. The integrator is sequential in the layer index — the history couples each layer to the previous few — so it cannot be vectorised across layers, and that is why it is a Python loop rather than an array expression.

def ttaup(t, tau, prad, pturb, grav):
    """Set up arrays + boundary seed for the log-pressure hydrostatic integration (Kurucz TTAUP)."""
    n = t.size

    # output arrays, filled layer by layer in the loop
    abstd  = np.zeros(n)            # opacity kappa_Ross at each layer (== 1 on the cold start)
    ptotal = np.zeros(n)           # total pressure  P_total = exp(log P)
    pgas   = np.zeros(n)           # gas pressure    P_gas   = P_total - P_rad - P_turb
    dlg_tau = np.log(tau[1] / tau[0]) if n > 1 else 0.0   # constant log-tau step (= ln10 * 0.125)

    # rolling history of log P (plog1..plog4) and of the derivative dplog (dplog1..dplog3)
    plog1 = plog2 = plog3 = plog4 = 0.0
    dplog1 = dplog2 = dplog3 = 0.0

    # seed opacity at the top layer (kappa_0 = 0.1) to set the boundary pressure
    abstd[0] = 0.1
    if prad[0] > 0.0:              # warm-restart guard; prad[0]==0 on the cold start, so no-op here
        abstd[0] = min(0.1, grav * tau[0] / max(prad[0], 1e-300) / 2.0)

    return _ttaup_loop(t, tau, prad, pturb, grav, n, abstd, ptotal, pgas, dlg_tau,
                       plog1, plog2, plog3, plog4, dplog1, dplog2, dplog3)

The second cell is the engine: the per-layer loop. It has to stay in one cell because it is a single function — the layer-to-layer history makes it inherently sequential, so it cannot be split or vectorised — but its three parts are marked by comment banners. For each layer jjj it runs the predictor (extrapolate ln⁡P\ln PlnP from the history), then the corrector inner loop (the evaluate-then-check ordering: store PtotalP_{\rm total}Ptotal​, gas pressure, opacity, and derivative first, then test convergence), then shifts the history forward by one. [8]Python 
def _ttaup_loop(t, tau, prad, pturb, grav, n, abstd, ptotal, pgas, dlg_tau,
                plog1, plog2, plog3, plog4, dplog1, dplog2, dplog3):
    for j in range(n):

        # ---- PREDICTOR: extrapolate log P from the history ----
        if j == 0:
            plog = np.log(max(grav / abstd[0] * tau[0], 1e-300))     # boundary: kappa_0 = 0.1
        elif j <= 3:
            plog = plog1 + dplog1                                     # short history: one-step
        else:
            plog = (3.0*plog4 + 8.0*dplog1 - 4.0*dplog2 + 8.0*dplog3) / 3.0   # 4-term multistep

        # ---- CORRECTOR loop: EVALUATE the stored values, THEN check convergence ----
        error, dplog, itn = 1.0, 0.0, 1
        while True:
            # evaluate and store pressure, gas pressure, opacity, and derivative from the trial plog
            plog = min(plog, 709.78)                       # guard exp() against overflow
            ptotal[j] = np.exp(plog)                       # total pressure at this layer
            pgas[j] = ptotal[j] + (prad[0] - prad[j]) - pturb[j]     # gas = total - P_rad run - P_turb
            if pgas[j] <= 0.0:                             # non-physical: clamp and stop this layer
                pgas[j] = 1e-30; abstd[j] = 0.1; break
            abstd[j] = 1.0                                 # cold-start opacity (empty ROSSTAB -> 1)
            dplog = grav / abstd[j] * tau[j] / ptotal[j] * dlg_tau   # d(log P) across one log-tau step

            # only now test convergence -- the stored values keep this trial plog
            itn += 1
            if itn > 1000 or error <= 5.0e-5:              # converged (or out of iterations): STOP
                break                                       # -> STORED values use this trial plog

            # corrector estimate, using the just-computed dplog:
            if j == 0:
                pnew = np.log(max(grav / abstd[j] * tau[j], 1e-300))
            elif j <= 3:
                pnew = (plog + 2.0*plog1 + dplog + dplog1) / 3.0
            else:
                pnew = (126.0*plog1 - 14.0*plog3 + 9.0*plog4
                        + 42.0*dplog + 108.0*dplog1 - 54.0*dplog2 + 24.0*dplog3) / 121.0
            error = abs(pnew - plog)                       # convergence test for the NEXT iteration
            plog = 0.5 * (pnew + plog)                     # average predictor and corrector

        # ---- shift the history forward one layer ----
        plog4, plog3, plog2, plog1 = plog3, plog2, plog1, plog
        dplog3, dplog2, dplog1 = dplog2, dplog1, dplog
    return abstd, ptotal, pgas

Running the integrator

With the temperature, the radiation pressure, and the opacity convention in hand, we run the integrator and form the two output quantities the structure carries: the gas pressure (already done inside the loop) and the column mass.

The gas-pressure line in the loop reads pgas = ptotal + (prad[0] - prad[j]) - pturb, and the + prad[0] is worth understanding rather than memorising. What the integrator actually accumulates in ptotal is the column weight g m=Ptotal(τ)−Ptotal(0)g\,m = P_{\rm total}(\tau) - P_{\rm total}(0)gm=Ptotal​(τ)−Ptotal​(0) — the pressure relative to the top boundary. Since the boundary carries no gas, Ptotal(0)P_{\rm total}(0)Ptotal​(0) is exactly the surface radiation pressure Prad(0)P_{\rm rad}(0)Prad​(0). To recover the proper gas pressure Pgas(τ)=Ptotal(τ)−Prad(τ)P_{\rm gas}(\tau) = P_{\rm total}(\tau) - P_{\rm rad}(\tau)Pgas​(τ)=Ptotal​(τ)−Prad​(τ) the code must therefore add the surface term back before subtracting the local one, which is precisely ptotal + P_rad(0) - P_rad(tau). On this cold start prad[0] is zero, so the line reduces to the plain Pgas=Ptotal−PradP_{\rm gas}=P_{\rm total}-P_{\rm rad}Pgas​=Ptotal​−Prad​, but the algebra is general. The column mass is then

m=Ptotalg,m = \frac{P_{\rm total}}{g},m=gPtotal​​,

the weight per unit area of everything above — the depth variable the .atm file is tabulated against and the one the opacity and transfer lectures integrate over.

What this atmosphere is not yet: radiative equilibrium

We have a structure that satisfies hydrostatic equilibrium — but only with a placeholder opacity κ≡1\kappa\equiv1κ≡1 and a grey temperature law that assumed wavelength-independent opacity. Neither is true of a real star. Lecture 10 takes the second structural constraint, radiative equilibrium: in a purely radiative atmosphere the radiative flux is constant with depth (no energy created or destroyed in the photosphere); where convection sets in, the conserved quantity is instead the sum of the radiative and convective fluxes. The grey temperature does not satisfy this once the opacity is wavelength-dependent, so the temperature is corrected, layer by layer, until the emergent flux matches σTeff4\sigma T_{\rm eff}^4σTeff4​ and the flux divergence vanishes. Feeding that corrected temperature back into this hydrostatic integration — now with the real Rosseland-mean opacity from the equation of state instead of κ≡1\kappa\equiv1κ≡1 — and iterating the two to convergence is how ATLAS builds a self-consistent model atmosphere.

That closes the loop the whole course has been tracing: parameters →\to→ atmosphere →\to→ spectrum. The first lecture took the atmosphere as given and we spent the synthesis half (Lectures 1-8) turning it into a spectrum to machine precision; this lecture began the inverse half by building the structure from TeffT_{\rm eff}Teff​ and log⁡g\log glogg; and Lecture 10 makes that structure self-consistent. With both halves in hand, a star's two numbers become its spectrum — from the standard grey-atmosphere approximations and the ATLAS numerical conventions, to the bit.

Synthesis

A model atmosphere is the run of temperature, pressure, and density with depth, fixed by hydrostatic and radiative equilibrium. This lecture built the hydrostatic half. Hydrostatic equilibrium in optical depth is dP/dτ=g/κdP/d\tau = g/\kappadP/dτ=g/κ, equivalently Ptotal=g mP_{\rm total} = g\,mPtotal​=gm for column mass mmm (up to the top-boundary constant) — the total pressure is the weight per area of the overlying gas. The grey/Hopf law supplies the temperature, and on the cold start the opacity is the crude placeholder κ≡1\kappa\equiv1κ≡1 (the empty Rosseland table), which gives the first-guess structure ATLAS then refines. Integrating the balance in log pressure with a predictor-corrector multistep — and reproducing the production code's evaluate-then-check ordering and its radiation-pressure subtraction — gives the gas pressure and column mass to machine precision, sharpening the first lecture's one-line P=gτP=g\tauP=gτ from one part in 10510^{5}105 to the last bit.
